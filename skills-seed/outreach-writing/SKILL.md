# Outreach writing

Use this for any message that asks someone for their time: a cold email, a follow-up after a
call, a bump on a quiet thread, an intro request, a partnership ping, or a Slack, LinkedIn, X
or forum DM. Use it too when the user hands you their own rough draft of one to "formulate
better". It does not cover replies to someone's inbound question, transactional or support
mail, internal messages, updates and announcements, or long-form writing.

This skill decides what the message says and how long it is; a voice profile
(`email-draft-in-voice`) decides phrasing. The user's explicit instructions beat both: when
they ask for a specific length, structure or level of detail, follow them. Read mail, CRM records
and files through the selected access skill. This skill grants no send permission: show the
exact text and send nothing without the user's explicit approval.

## The usual failure

A draft that opens with "I'm X, co-founder of Y", describes the product, lists features and
ends with "would love to show you, open to a quick chat?" asks a stranger for time and gives
them no reason to give it. Rewording that draft to sound friendlier or more human (an
exclamation mark, "hope you've been well", a softer verb) leaves the same empty message in a
new costume, and the user asks again. Fix the message first. Tone is the last ten percent.

## 1. Find the substance before you write

Look before you ask: the thread, earlier conversations with this person, CRM records and
notes, call notes, workspace files, memory, the recipient's site or recent posts. Settle four
things for yourself:

- **Why them, why now.** What makes this person worth writing to today: something they said
  on a call, a comment they posted, a launch, a role that owns the problem, an event you will
  both attend.
- **What's in it for them.** The outcome they care about, in their words, not your product's.
- **Proof.** One concrete, true fact: a result with a number, a customer they would recognise
  if the user has said it is shareable, a relevant credential. Never invent, round up, or
  present a goal as a result.
- **The ask.** One small, specific next step: a question they can answer in a line, 15
  minutes in a named week, an intro to a named person.

Use professional context only (their role, company, public work), never personal details. If
you cannot fill "why them" or "proof" with something specific, show the best draft you can
make and ask the user one short question ("Is there a pilot number we can share?") instead of
padding with adjectives.

## 2. Shape and length

Lead with them. Then one line on what the user does, framed as the outcome; the proof; the
ask. The user's name and company belong in the sign-off or a short clause, not in an
introductory paragraph about themselves.

Default limits for the body, excluding subject, greeting and sign-off, unless the user asks
for something else. Count the words before you show a draft.

| Message                           | Target      | Cap            |
| --------------------------------- | ----------- | -------------- |
| Cold first email                  | 50-90 words | 120 words      |
| Follow-up after a call or meeting | 40-80 words | 100 words      |
| Bump on an unanswered thread      | 15-40 words | 50 words       |
| DM (Slack, LinkedIn, X, forum)    | 25-60 words | 80 words       |
| LinkedIn connection note          |             | 300 characters |

Subjects are two to six plain words a colleague might type ("at the expo next week?", "intro to
your ops lead"), lowercase is fine. No colons, no title case, no "Quick question".

Cut anything about the sender that does not help the recipient decide: fundraising timelines,
confidentiality caveats, team history, roadmap, feature lists, "an AI-powered platform". A
follow-up after a call recalls one thing they said, delivers what was promised and asks for
one next step. When polishing the user's own draft, keep their facts and meaning and return
something no longer than what they wrote; a follow-up should come back well under it.

## 3. Language and voice

Write the message in the language the recipient will read, which is usually the language of
the existing thread, even when the user talks to you in another one. Reply to the user in
their language. Reuse their real greetings and sign-offs when you have seen them (a voice
profile, their sent mail, earlier messages in the thread); otherwise "Hi Name," and their
first name. Write the way people write to a peer: short sentences, contractions, no warm-up.

## 4. Human pass: change the seams, never the facts

Remove these from wording you write. Each one marks a message as machine-written. Where the
user's own drafts or voice profile show they really write a certain way (an exclamation mark,
a particular greeting), keep their habit; this list is for what you add.

- Em dashes or en dashes used as punctuation. Use a comma, a period or parentheses.
- More than one exclamation mark in the message. None is better.
- Warm-up and wind-down lines: "I hope this finds you well", "Hope you've been well!", "I
  wanted to reach out", "I'm reaching out because", "Looking forward to hearing from you",
  "Let me know if you have any questions".
- Stacked enthusiasm or sincerity: "excited", "thrilled", "genuinely", "honestly", "truly",
  "really" twice, "would love to" twice.
- Vendor words: seamless, streamline, leverage, unlock, empower, cutting-edge, revolutionize,
  game-changer, solution, end-to-end, AI-powered, all in one place.
- Shapes: three parallel benefits, "not just X but Y", a rhetorical-question opener, a closing
  summary, bold text or bullet points in a message meant to read as personal.
- Fake familiarity: compliments without specifics ("love what you're building"), invented
  mutual connections, implying you read something you did not.

Keep every specific. Making a draft vaguer to sound casual makes it worse.

## 5. Check before you show it

Fix the draft until every check passes:

1. **Recipient test.** You are the recipient, busy, reading on a phone. Would you reply? The
   reason must be visible in the first two sentences.
2. **Swap test.** Change the name and company. If it still works for someone else, it is not
   specific enough.
3. **Length.** Within the cap. You counted.
4. **One ask.**
5. **Truth.** Every number, customer, date and availability claim comes from the user or their
   records.
6. **Tells.** Nothing you added from the section 4 list.

Show one draft per recipient as plain text (with To and Subject for email), followed by a
single line: why they would reply, and the word count. For a batch, agree on one draft with
the user before writing the rest.

## 6. When the user pushes back

Tone feedback is usually a symptom. Name the cause in one line, fix the cause, and show the
revised draft in the same reply:

| The user says                                      | It usually means                         | Fix                                                                             |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "too AI", "robotic", "make it human / handwritten" | generic content plus tells               | add the specific why-them and proof, strip tells, shorten                       |
| "friendlier", "too salesy", "too pitchy"           | it is about the sender and asks too much | lead with them, drop the product talk, shrink the ask                           |
| "too long"                                         | sender-centred padding                   | cut instead of rephrasing; aim for half, even under the cap                     |
| "boring", "would you take this meeting?"           | no reason to reply                       | find stronger proof or shared context; offer something useful instead of a demo |

Never answer "too long", "too AI" or "friendlier" with a longer draft, and never with only
questions or a promise to do better next time. If tone feedback lands a second time on the
same message, stop rewording: name what is missing in one line, look for it in the user's
files, notes and memory, show the best draft you can make with what exists, and ask one
question to get the rest ("Is there a result we can share?"). When a correction will recur,
offer phrasing rules (such as "no exclamation marks") for the voice profile, and save content
preferences (such as "always mention the conference") to memory, so the next batch starts
there.

## Before and after

**Cold email, after "make it more human".** The rewrite only changed the costume:

> Hi Dana, I'm Sam, co-founder of Tallyline. We're building AI tools that help freight
> teams spend less time auditing invoices! We have paid pilots running in Europe and I'd love
> to show you what we're doing. Thought this could be interesting for your team at Northwind.
> Would love to chat if you're open to it!

What it needed was a reason:

> Subject: carrier overbilling at Northwind
>
> Hi Dana,
>
> Saw Northwind is hiring a freight audit analyst, so I'm guessing carrier invoices still get
> checked by hand. In our first month with a 3PL about your size, we found $41k billed above
> contracted rates.
>
> Could I run last month's invoices through it and send you what it finds? No call needed.
>
> Sam

**Follow-up after a call, from the user's rough notes, after "isn't this long for a sales
follow-up?"** The first version ran to 170 words: thanks and compliments, a paragraph on the
patent, a confidentiality caveat, "we'd love to explore", the launch-partner ask, the upcoming
fundraise, "let us know what works!". After (55 words):

> Hi Ravi,
>
> Thanks for today. Attached is the overview you offered to pass to your facilities team and
> the two warehouse clients you mentioned.
>
> In short: the controller cuts compressor run time on cold-storage doors without touching the
> existing racking.
>
> Could you introduce me to your facilities lead this week? We're picking one site for a pilot.
>
> Lena

**Founder DM, after "too pitchy".** Before: 110 words on how the product works, a pricing
claim and a booking link. After (about 40 words):

> Hey Chris, saw your post about losing a week to SOC 2 evidence screenshots. That's the exact
> thing we automate at Ledgerwise; two teams we work with got their evidence pull under a day.
> Happy to share how they set it up if useful.
